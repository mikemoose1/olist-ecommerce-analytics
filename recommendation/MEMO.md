# Late deliveries are linked to about a quarter of the bad Olist reviews: what to test

Written for an e-commerce operations or marketplace manager. Every number comes from `recommendation/analyze.py`. Each one carries an invisible tag naming the fact it states, and `make verify` fails if a number has no tag, if the tag names a fact with a different value, or if a tag follows no number, so changing or swapping a number without changing the fact behind it is caught. The orders are single-seller orders bought 2017-01<!--f:window_first_month--> to 2018-08<!--f:window_last_month--> and delivered, 94,931<!--f:orders--> in all, as defined in `ontology/ontology.yml`.

## What it is

6.87<!--f:late_rate-->% of these orders (6,518<!--f:late_orders-->) arrived after the date Olist promised. Late orders average 2.27<!--f:stars_raw_late--> stars. On-time orders average 4.31<!--f:stars_raw_on_time-->.

## Why it matters

**Stars.** Late orders score about 2<!--f:stars_matched_rounded--> stars lower than on-time orders, and about 1.6<!--f:abs:stars_hold_1dp--> lower in the most recent months. Compared with on-time orders in the same state, product category, freight band and month, late orders score 1.96<!--f:abs:stars_matched--> stars lower (95<!--d:ci_level-->% range 1.91<!--f:abs:stars_matched_hi--> to 2.01<!--f:abs:stars_matched_lo-->). A regression with the same controls gives 1.97<!--f:abs:stars_reg--> (1.92<!--f:abs:stars_reg_hi--> to 2.02<!--f:abs:stars_reg_lo-->). Stars fall with each extra day for about two weeks: 3.74<!--f:days_1 day--> at 1<!--d:bucket_1_day--> day late, 2.94<!--f:days_2-3 days--> at 2-3<!--d:bucket_2_3_days--> days, 2.10<!--f:days_4-7 days--> at 4-7<!--d:bucket_4_7_days--> days and 1.67<!--f:days_8-14 days--> at 8-14<!--d:bucket_8_14_days--> days. After that they level off (1.72<!--f:days_15+ days--> at 15<!--d:bucket_15_plus_days--> days or more). Much of that slope is a change in who answered when: in the 1<!--d:bucket_1_day--> day group only 0.7<!--f:before_pct_1 day-->% of surveys went out before delivery, in the 8-14<!--d:bucket_8_14_days--> day group 99.9<!--f:before_pct_8-14 days-->%, and surveys sent before delivery score lower at every lateness from 1<!--f:survey_same_days_min--> to 8<!--f:survey_same_days_max--> days (see the survey-timing note below). That per-day comparison is thin at the edges: 3<!--f:survey_thin_days--> of those 8<!--f:survey_days_n--> days have fewer than 10<!--d:thin_cell--> orders in one of the two groups, as few as 1<!--f:survey_thin_min_n-->.

**Bad reviews.** 62.4<!--f:low_star_late_pct-->% of late orders get 1<!--d:star_low_min--> or 2<!--d:star_low_max--> stars, against 8.7<!--f:low_star_on_time_pct-->% of on-time orders. Late delivery is the estimated cause of about 3,110<!--f:c_cost_causal--> of the 11,635<!--f:c_low_star_all--> one- and two-star reviews on these orders, about 27<!--f:c_cost_causal_share-->% of them. The estimate compares each late order with the same seller's orders that arrived just on time, in the same product category and month. So a seller's or product category's usual quality is held fixed. Six reasonable ways of making that comparison give 2,880<!--f:c_cost_range_lo--> to 3,350<!--f:c_cost_range_hi--> reviews. If hidden seller sloppiness sat inside every late order, it would explain about 150<!--f:c_cost_bias_hi--> of them at the top of its 95<!--d:ci_level-->% range, which is not a hard cap. This is an estimate from past orders, not a randomized test. By days late, the share of bad reviews follows a ramp. This is descriptive: it is the raw share for each day, and part of the climb is survey timing (see How sure we are). Up to the promised day, 8.7<!--f:c_ramp_on_time-->% of reviews are 1<!--d:star_low_min--> or 2<!--d:star_low_max--> stars. Once the order is late, that share climbs about 11<!--f:c_ramp_slope--> points a day and levels off near 78<!--f:c_ramp_plateau-->% from day 6<!--f:c_ramp_end_day--> after the promise. So the pattern shows no cliff at the promised day. The data cannot say how much of the climb is the delay itself and how much is survey timing.

**Repeat buying shows no clear effect.** Customers whose first order arrived late were somewhat less likely to order again (about 1.0<!--f:c_rep_late_1dp-->% vs 1.3<!--f:c_rep_on_time_1dp-->% within 90<!--d:repeat_window_days--> days), but repeat buying on Olist is so rare that this gap is too small to measure reliably, and fixing lateness would be worth at most a few thousand reais in repeat sales, so we make no revenue claim. More precisely, of people whose first order was late, 1.02<!--f:rep_late-->% came back within 90<!--d:repeat_window_days--> days, against 1.30<!--f:rep_on_time-->% for on-time first orders. After matching, the gap is -0.10<!--f:rep_matched--> points, with a range of -0.52<!--f:rep_matched_lo--> to 0.22<!--f:rep_matched_hi-->. That range includes zero, so the data cannot tell a real drop from chance. Ever coming back gives the same answer (-0.43<!--f:repever_matched--> points, range -1.03<!--f:repever_lo--> to 0.22<!--f:repever_hi-->).

**Money.** Over the last 12<!--f:last12_n--> months of the window, lost repeat sales come to R$0<!--f:money12_low--> (low), R$1,000<!--f:money12_mid--> (mid) and R$4,500<!--f:money12_high--> (high), rounded to the nearest five hundred reais because the middle figure moves by about a hundred with small changes in how matches are built. The high figure is the top of the 95<!--d:ci_level-->% range, not a hard cap. That is 5,586<!--f:late_first_last12--> late first orders in those months, times the repeat drop, times R$163<!--f:repeat_spend-->, which is what a returning customer spends in their 90<!--d:repeat_window_days--> days. It is small because few customers come back at all. Late orders carried R$1.0 million<!--f:late_gmv_last12--> of sales in those months, 8.6<!--f:late_gmv_last12_pct-->% of all sales. That money went through a bad experience. It was not lost. Olist's commission and the sales value of a star are not in this data. So the case for fixing lateness rests on ratings, not on repeat revenue.

## Where late orders cluster

**When.** 48.3<!--f:top3_months_late-->% of late orders came from three months that had 21.7<!--f:top3_months_orders-->% of orders: 2018-03 (19.2<!--f:month_2018-03-->% late), 2018-02 (14.2<!--f:month_2018-02-->%) and 2017-11 (12.5<!--f:month_2017-11-->%). The spike hit the whole country. Outside Rio de Janeiro, orders ran 9.8<!--f:rest_spike-->% late from 2017-11<!--f:spike_first_month--> to 2018-03<!--f:spike_last_month--> and 4.1<!--f:rest_other-->% in other months.

**Lines to steer by.** Three lines on the monthly late rate tell a manager when to act. The alarm line sits at 8.38<!--f:c_alarm_line-->% for a typical month of 6,372<!--f:c_typical_orders--> orders, and higher for smaller months. A month above it is a real change, not bad luck. Three months crossed it: 2017-11<!--f:c_flagged_1-->, 2018-02<!--f:c_flagged_2--> and 2018-03<!--f:c_flagged_3-->. The line is set from how much the late rate normally swings from month to month, which is far more than chance alone would give, so it sits higher than a textbook limit. The goal is 4.4<!--f:c_goal_line-->%, the median of the 15<!--f:c_stable_months--> stable months (4.43<!--f:c_goal_exact-->%), so half of them already beat it. "Stable" means not an extreme month. It does not mean healthy. A stretch line at 3.4<!--f:c_stretch_line-->% (3.43<!--f:c_stretch_exact-->%) shows the middle month if late seller handoffs ran late no more often than on-time ones: a what-if, not a tested cause. It is the same measure as the goal, the median of the same stable months, so the gap between them is a median-scale read of the what-if, about 1.0<!--f:c_whatif_gap--> point (single months range about 0.2<!--f:c_whatif_gap_lo--> to 2.9<!--f:c_whatif_gap_hi-->). Over the whole window the same what-if takes the late rate from 6.87<!--f:late_rate-->% to 5.49<!--f:c_whatif_rate-->%. The carrier may have been late anyway.

**Where.** North and northeast states receive 11.3<!--f:nne_share_orders-->% of orders and 20.0<!--f:nne_share_late-->% of late orders, a 12.2<!--f:nne_rate-->% late rate. The worst are AL (21.6<!--f:state_AL-->%), MA (17.7<!--f:state_MA-->%) and SE (15.5<!--f:state_SE-->%). Rio de Janeiro had 22.9<!--f:rj_share_late-->% of late orders, almost all from the spike. It ran 26.6<!--f:rj_spike-->% late from 2017-11<!--f:spike_first_month--> to 2018-03<!--f:spike_last_month--> and 4.4<!--f:rj_other-->% in other months.

**Who.** On 9.0<!--f:handoff_late_share-->% of orders the seller handed the parcel to the carrier after its ship-by date. Those orders were late 21.2<!--f:late_if_handoff_late-->% of the time, against 5.4<!--f:late_if_handoff_ok-->% when the handoff was on time. 27.8<!--f:seller_side_pct-->% of late orders had a late handoff. The rest were handed over on time and still arrived late. A short list of bad sellers is not the answer. Among sellers with 20<!--d:min_seller_orders--> or more orders, the 73<!--f:dbl_n--> that run late at least twice as often as average carry 12.7<!--f:dbl_share_late-->% of late orders.

## What to do: two tests

1. **Hold sellers to their ship-by date.** Pick half of sellers at random. They get a reminder the day before each ship-by date and a visible on-time handoff score. The other half see no change. Assign by seller, not by order. Measure the share of handoffs after ship-by first, then the late rate and average stars, over 8<!--d:test_weeks--> weeks. The ceiling is about 1,300<!--f:avoidable_late--> fewer late orders (20.6<!--f:avoidable_share-->% of them), if late-handoff orders became as reliable as on-time ones. The real gain will be lower, since sellers who miss ship-by may differ in other ways.
2. **Add buffer days to the promise where and when lateness is likely.** For north and northeast addresses, and in months that look like this window's highest-lateness months, add buffer days to the promised date on a random half of orders at checkout. 28.6<!--f:late_within_3-->% of late orders were 1-3<!--d:near_miss_days--> days late and 56.1<!--f:late_within_7-->% were within 7<!--d:week_days--> days, so a few days would turn many of them into on-time orders. Measure average stars and checkout conversion first; the late rate is a weak readout here, because a longer promise lowers it by definition. Conversion is not in this data and is the main risk. Longer promises may also cost stars: on-time orders with 36<!--d:promise_long_days-->+ day promises averaged 4.17<!--f:promise_long_stars--> stars, against 4.42<!--f:promise_short_stars--> for 0-14<!--d:promise_short_days--> days. That raw comparison also mixes in distance.

## How sure we are

- The star penalty held in 2018-05<!--f:holdout_first_month--> to 2018-08<!--f:holdout_last_month-->, the months set aside before measuring, but it was smaller: 1.56<!--f:abs:stars_hold--> stars (range 1.44<!--f:abs:stars_hold_hi--> to 1.68<!--f:abs:stars_hold_lo-->) against 2.05<!--f:abs:stars_disc--> earlier. Late orders in those months were less late (median 5<!--f:med_late_hold--> days against 7<!--f:med_late_disc-->), which fits the day-by-day pattern.
- North and northeast stayed worse (13.8<!--f:nne_disc-->% against 6.9<!--f:rest_disc-->% elsewhere, then 6.8<!--f:nne_hold-->% against 4.2<!--f:rest_hold-->%). Late handoffs stayed risky (21.0<!--f:handoff_disc_late-->% against 6.3<!--f:handoff_disc_ok-->%, then 22.0<!--f:handoff_hold_late-->% against 3.0<!--f:handoff_hold_ok-->%).
- Some of the star gap is associated with customers rating while still waiting. Olist sends the survey on the promised date if the parcel has not arrived. 74.5<!--f:survey_before_pct-->% of late orders' surveys went out before delivery and average 1.84<!--f:stars_sent_before--> stars. Late orders surveyed after delivery average 3.53<!--f:stars_sent_after-->. These two groups also differ in how late the parcel was: orders surveyed after delivery were 1.9<!--f:days_late_sent_after--> days late on average, those surveyed before delivery 13.5<!--f:days_late_sent_before-->. Comparing only orders that were equally late (each day from 1<!--f:survey_same_days_min--> to 8<!--f:survey_same_days_max--> days late, where both groups exist), surveys sent before delivery still score 1.27<!--f:survey_gap_same_days--> stars lower. That is about 70<!--f:survey_gap_share_lo-->% to 78<!--f:survey_gap_share_hi-->% of the raw 1.69<!--f:survey_gap_raw-->-star gap, depending on how the days are weighted (75<!--f:survey_gap_same_days_pct-->% when every order counts the same). Among orders only 1-3<!--d:near_miss_days--> days late the gap is 1.15<!--f:survey_near_gap--> stars (2.38<!--f:survey_near_before--> before delivery, 3.53<!--f:survey_near_after--> after). So the star gap goes with survey timing as well as with how late the parcel was. Survey timing may still go with other differences between these orders, so this data cannot say how much of the gap is timing. It does not show that sending the survey later would raise scores, so that is not on the list. The estimate of about 3,110<!--f:c_cost_causal--> extra bad reviews includes this survey-before-delivery channel: all of its extra bad reviews were written before the parcel arrived, and among reviews written after delivery the estimate is below zero. So it counts customers who rated while still waiting, not only customers who rated after a late parcel arrived.
- Matching found on-time look-alikes for 87.2<!--f:matched_pct-->% of reviewed late orders (85.2<!--f:matched_pct_all_late-->% of all late orders: the 152<!--f:late_unreviewed_n--> late orders with no review have no stars to match on). This is the matching behind the star gap above, not the same-seller estimate. It controls for (coarse) state, category, freight and month, so it cannot rule out product or seller quality. The same-seller estimate holds seller and category fixed, but it is still not a randomized test. That is why both actions are tests, not rollouts.
